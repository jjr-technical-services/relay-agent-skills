# Safe, trauma-informed facilitation

## Principles

SAMHSA's concept of a trauma-informed approach names six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; and cultural, historical and gender issues. In a workshop they translate to:

| Principle | In the session |
|---|---|
| Safety | Content note, a way to step away, a support person available where possible, no surprise content |
| Trustworthiness and transparency | Say what the session will and will not cover; share the agenda |
| Peer support | Where appropriate, include people with lived experience who are prepared and supported |
| Collaboration and mutuality | Ask for input on examples; invite questions |
| Empowerment, voice and choice | Every activity has an opt-out; participation options include anonymous ones |
| Cultural, historical and gender issues | Examples reflect the audience; avoid stereotypes; acknowledge different help-seeking norms |

Psychological safety in a group means people can ask questions and make mistakes in practice without embarrassment. Set norms early and model them.

## Content notes

Read aloud and show on screen at the start, and include in the pre-session email. Include: the topics covered in plain words, that participants may step away or turn off their camera at any time without explanation, that no one will be asked to share personal experiences, and where to find support during and after the session (the resource slide and any on-site support person). Avoid the word "trigger" as a label when a plain description works.

## Safe messaging

When content touches suicide, self-harm, or mental-health crises, follow recognized safe-messaging practice, such as the Recommendations for Reporting on Suicide and the Framework for Successful Messaging from the National Action Alliance for Suicide Prevention. Use the organization's own guidance where it has one. Core points:

- No detail on method or location.
- Do not present suicide as a solution, as inevitable, or as caused by a single event.
- No sensational, dramatic, or romanticized language or images.
- Include help-seeking, recovery, and hope; show that support exists and how to reach it.
- Use "died by suicide", "attempted suicide", or "suicide attempt"; never "committed suicide", "successful" or "failed" attempt.
- Do not show statistics as shock value; if the organization provides data, present it with context and resources.

## Lived-experience stories

- The speaker is prepared: they have chosen what to share, reviewed it against safe-messaging practice with the organization, and have support before and after.
- The story focuses on the path to help and what helped, not on the worst moment. No method detail.
- The speaker has consented to how the story is used, including recording. Stories involving minors need parental consent and the organization's review.
- Offer the audience a pause afterwards.
- If these conditions cannot be met, replace the live story with a short, approved, written or recorded account, or a fictional composite labeled as fictional.

## What the session does not do

- **No screening or self-assessment of symptoms.** Screening tools are for clinical settings with follow-up. A live quiz can distress participants and creates a duty to respond the facilitator cannot meet. Replace with a knowledge check about signs and resources, or a private reflection on who the participant could turn to.
- **No diagnosis, advice about treatment, or counseling** during or after the session. Facilitators connect people to resources.

## Resource slide

- Place the organization-approved crisis resource first, using the organization's approved copy verbatim. If none is supplied, use the slot ORGANIZATION-APPROVED CRISIS RESOURCE (insert approved wording) and ask for it before delivery. Never type a crisis line, number, or text code from memory.
- In the US, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is reachable by call or text at 988. Use the organization's wording where it has one.
- Add host-institution resources (counseling center, student support, employee assistance program) as slots for the client to confirm.
- Leave the slide on screen during the measure and close, and include it in the follow-up email.

## Responding to hard moments

| Moment | Facilitator response |
|---|---|
| A participant discloses personal distress in the group | Thank them, acknowledge briefly, avoid probing, offer to connect after the session, and follow the organization's and host institution's procedure |
| A participant appears distressed or leaves | The support person or second facilitator checks in privately, following the procedure; the session continues |
| Someone may be at immediate risk | Follow the organization's emergency procedure and the host's; this is not handled by the facilitator alone |
| A question goes beyond scope (diagnosis, medication, a specific person's situation) | Say that it is outside what the session covers and point to the appropriate resource |
| A stigmatizing or harmful comment | Address it calmly, restate the norm, reframe with accurate language |

## When the community has had a recent loss

After a death by suicide in the community, sessions need extra care: coordinate with the host institution's response plan, avoid discussing the specific death, keep the focus on support and resources, and consider whether a workshop is the right step at that time.
