# Writing each section

## Need statement

A strong need statement is local, specific, current, cited, and connected to what the program does.

Structure:

1. **The situation in the community served**, with population-level data for the actual geography and age group, cited with source and year.
2. **The gap**: what exists now, who is not reached, and why (access, cost, awareness, language, stigma, capacity).
3. **Evidence from the organization's own work**, aggregated: demand, waitlists, partner requests, participant feedback. Label unaudited internal data as such.
4. **Why now**: a change in conditions, a partner's readiness, or a policy change, with its source.
5. **The bridge**: one or two sentences linking the gap to the program described next.

Rules:

- Never invent or round up a statistic. When no local figure exists, say so and use the nearest cited figure with its scope stated (for example, a state figure used for a county program, labelled as such).
- Prefer recent data; state the year of every figure. Flag any figure older than the organization's freshness rule.
- Avoid deficit-only framing. Describe the community's strengths and the barriers it faces, in the organization's own audience terms.
- For mental-health topics, describe what was measured (for example, the share of surveyed students who reported they would not know where to seek help) rather than implying diagnosis.
- Suicide-related content follows safe messaging practice: use "died by suicide", omit method and location, avoid single-cause explanations and sensational language, and include help-seeking and hope. Refer to the Recommendations for Reporting on Suicide and the Framework for Successful Messaging from the National Action Alliance for Suicide Prevention by name when the user asks for the basis; do not quote them from memory.

## Program design

Answer, in this order: who is served (eligibility, number, how recruited), what happens (activities, dosage and frequency, curriculum or approach), where, when (timeline by quarter or month), by whom (roles, qualifications, partners and their commitments), and why this approach (evidence base, prior results, participant input).

Keep every activity consistent with the budget, the logic model, and the evaluation plan. If the program uses an approach from published research, cite it and say how faithfully it will be delivered and what is adapted.

## SMART objectives

Each objective has: a measure, a baseline, a target, a date, and a data source.

`By DATE, NUMBER OR PERCENT of POPULATION will MEASURED CHANGE, as measured by INSTRUMENT OR RECORD, from a baseline of VALUE (SOURCE).`

Replace each capitalized placeholder; never leave placeholders in brackets, because some review systems misread bracketed text.

Illustrative (hypothetical round numbers): "By the end of the grant year, at least 400 students at partner campuses will complete the two-session workshop series, as recorded in attendance logs (baseline: 250 completed last year)." "At least 60% of completing students will report increased confidence in knowing where to seek help, measured by the pre- and post-workshop survey (baseline to be set in the first quarter)."

Check each objective:

- Measurable with data the organization actually collects or will collect.
- Achievable given staffing and budget; show the arithmetic when volumes are involved (for example, 20 workshops × 20 students = 400).
- Non-clinical: participation, knowledge, self-reported confidence, help-seeking intentions, connections to resources, satisfaction. Never reductions in diagnosis, symptoms, or suicide rates attributed to the program.
- Distinguish outputs (what the program does and how many take part) from outcomes (what changes for participants).

## Logic model and evaluation hand-offs

- Send activities, outputs, outcomes, and assumptions to build-logic-model; paste its result into the funder's form or table.
- Send objectives, measures, and data sources to design-program-evaluation; summarize its plan in the funder's evaluation section: questions, measures, instruments, data collection schedule, who analyses, how results will be used, and privacy protections.

## Organizational capacity

- Mission and track record relevant to this program, with results and their sources.
- Staff and contractor qualifications for the named roles; say how contractor roles are supervised.
- Partnerships with commitment evidence (letters, agreements).
- Financial management: systems, audit or review status, experience managing restricted grants.
- Data and privacy practices relevant to participant information.

## Sustainability

Reviewers look for realism. Name the sources that will continue the work (earned revenue such as fees paid by institutions, other funders already committed or in pipeline, partner absorption, unrestricted fundraising), what share each could cover, and what would scale down if they do not materialize. Do not claim that one year of funding will make a program permanently self-sustaining without evidence.

## Budget narrative

Hand to build-grant-budget. Then read the narrative and budget side by side: every activity has a cost line and every cost line has an activity; staff effort matches the program design; numbers match between sections.
