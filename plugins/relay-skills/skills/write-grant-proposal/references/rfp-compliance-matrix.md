# Parsing the RFP into a compliance matrix

## Why a matrix first

Reviewers and grants staff often screen for compliance before reading for merit, and some portals reject a submission outright when a limit or attachment is wrong. A matrix built before drafting makes every requirement visible, assigns it a home in the proposal, and gives a single place to track status.

## Where requirements hide

Read every document the funder supplies, in full. Requirements commonly sit in:

- the main narrative instructions (section headings, questions, limits);
- eligibility sections and FAQs (organization type, budget size, audit requirements, geography, years in operation, prior-grantee rules);
- the scoring rubric (what earns points, and sometimes required content not repeated elsewhere);
- formatting instructions (font, size, margins, spacing, page numbering, file type, file naming);
- attachment lists (board list, determination letter, audited financials, budget template, letters of support or commitment, résumés, organizational chart, logic model form);
- submission instructions (portal, deadline with time zone, who may submit, registration steps that take days);
- footnotes, addenda, webinar notes, and question-and-answer postings, which can change earlier rules;
- the award terms (reporting, match, indirect limits) that the proposal must be consistent with.

Addenda override the original; note the addendum date in the source column.

## How to count limits

- **Words**: confirm whether headings and references count. When the portal counts, the portal's count wins.
- **Characters**: confirm whether spaces count. "Characters including spaces" is common in portals; if unstated, assume spaces count and label the assumption.
- **Pages**: confirm whether attachments, tables, and references count toward the page limit.

Show the count after each section in the draft.

## Matrix format

| Requirement | Funder source | Limit or rule | Where answered | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eligibility: independent audit for the most recent fiscal year | Guidelines, Eligibility, item 3 | Required to apply | Attachment 4 | Eligibility risk |
| Statement of Need | Application question 2 | 2,000 characters including spaces | Draft section 2 | Met |
| Evaluation plan with measures and data sources | Scoring rubric, criterion C (30 points) | None stated | Draft section 5 | Partly met |
| Board list with affiliations | Attachments list, item 2 | PDF | Attachment 2 | Gap |
| Deadline | Guidelines, Key dates | Date and time with time zone as stated | Submission plan | Met |

(All rows illustrative.)

Status values:

- **Met**: answered and within the rule.
- **Partly met**: answered but incomplete, over the limit, or missing evidence.
- **Gap**: not yet answered or attached.
- **Eligibility risk**: the organization may not meet an eligibility rule. Always list these first.

## Handling eligibility risks

1. Quote the rule exactly.
2. State the organization's actual position (for example, "the most recent year had a financial review, not an audit").
3. Check whether the funder offers an alternative (some accept a review or reviewed statements below a budget threshold, or allow a fiscal sponsor).
4. Write the exact question for the program officer.
5. Advise the user not to invest in drafting until the answer is known, unless the deadline makes parallel work necessary; say which parts are reusable either way.

## Mapping scoring criteria

For each scoring criterion, record points available, the section that earns them, and the evidence that shows it. Allocate words roughly in proportion to points unless the funder sets section limits. A criterion worth 30 points answered in two sentences is a common and avoidable loss.

## Required headings

Use the funder's exact headings, capitalization, and numbering. If the organization's standard template calls the section "The Problem" and the funder asks for "Statement of Need", the proposal uses "Statement of Need". Reviewers score with the rubric beside them and look for their own words.
