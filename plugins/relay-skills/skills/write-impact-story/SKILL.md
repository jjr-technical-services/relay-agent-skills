# Write Impact Story

Turn real experiences into stories that show what a program makes possible while
protecting the people in them. Impact stories go wrong in predictable ways: a quote is
used without the person's current permission, a minor's story runs on the young
person's own say-so, details make someone identifiable in a small community, a composite
is presented as a real person, or the story is framed as the organization rescuing a
helpless person, sometimes with clinical claims the program never measured. This skill
puts consent and privacy first through a story bank, then writes strengths-based stories
in the participant's voice for each format.

## Inputs to gather

- **Source material**: program notes, interview transcripts, submissions, survey
  comments. Note who collected each and when.
- **Consent records** for each person: what they agreed to (which uses, channels, name
  or no name, photo or no photo), when, in what form, and any expiry or withdrawal.
  If a record is not supplied, treat consent as missing, not as given.
- **Age** of each person at the time of the story and at the time consent was given.
- **The organization's story policy**: consent form, who reviews stories (program lead,
  safety reviewer), rules for minors, composites, and photos.
- **Target formats and uses**: social post, newsletter feature, grant or funder report
  vignette, speech excerpt, appeal letter; audience and length for each.
- **What the program measured**: participation, knowledge, self-reported confidence,
  help-seeking intentions. Outcome language must stay within these.

Never invent a person, quote, detail, or outcome. If a story needs a detail that is not
in the material, write `To confirm with participant` and list it. When an input such as
the organization's story policy or a consent record is missing, proceed on the strictest
reasonable assumption (treat consent as missing) and label every assumption you make.

## Workflow

1. **Build the story bank first.** One row per person or story, before any drafting.
   Read references/consent-and-privacy.md for the status rules. Assign exactly one
   consent status:
   - **Consent on file**: documented consent that covers this use, this channel, and
     this level of identification, not expired or withdrawn; for a minor, includes a
     parent or guardian's consent and the organization's review.
   - **Needs consent**: no record, verbal only, consent that does not cover this use
     or channel, expired consent, or a minor without parent or guardian consent. Do not
     draft for publication; draft only a version to show the person for approval.
   - **Do not use**: the person withdrew or declined, the material came from a setting
     where sharing was never expected (a support session, a crisis conversation), or
     the risk of harm or identification cannot be managed. Do not quote, paraphrase,
     or use as composite material.

2. **Check the minor rules.** Anyone under 18 when the material was collected or when
   it will be published needs a parent or guardian's written consent and the young
   person's own agreement, plus the organization's designated review before use. Use
   first name only or a changed name, no school, town, or photo that identifies them,
   and a shorter retention of the story. Read references/consent-and-privacy.md.

3. **De-identify.** Remove or change anything that could identify someone in their
   community: full name, school or campus, small town, rare job, specific dates, unique
   events, identifying photos, and health or diagnosis details. Changed names are
   noted to the reviewer. Consider whether details combined could identify the person
   even when each alone could not.

4. **Decide on composites.** A composite combines experiences from several consented
   people into one illustrative story. Use only material with consent that allows it,
   label it every time it appears ("This story is a composite of several participants'
   experiences"), never give it a real photo, and never attach a real person's quote to
   the composite.

5. **Choose the story shape.** Read references/story-craft.md. Use a strengths-based
   arc: the person and what they care about, the challenge in context (systems and
   barriers, not personal deficits), what they did and what the program offered, what
   changed in their own words and within what was measured, and what comes next. Do not
   open with the person at their lowest, do not contrast a "before" at the lowest point with
   an "after" of rescue, and do not make the organization or the donor the hero.

6. **Use the participant's voice.** Prefer direct quotes over narration. Edit quotes
   only for length, marking cuts, and never change meaning. Every quote goes to the
   person for quote approval before publication, including any trimmed version.

7. **Keep claims inside what was measured.** No clinical claims: do not say the program
   brought about a cure, treated, healed, fixed, or prevented a condition, or saved a life. Describe
   what the person did and said and what the program measured.

8. **Route sensitive content.** If the story touches suicide, self-harm, or a crisis,
   run it through write-safe-mental-health-content and use the
   `ORGANIZATION-APPROVED CRISIS RESOURCE (insert approved wording)` slot. If the story will be cut into many
   channel versions, classify those adaptations with repurpose-content.

9. **Write each format** and assemble the output below.

## Output

### Output contract

Use these section headings verbatim, as Markdown headings, in this order:

1. Story bank
2. Consent actions
3. Drafts
4. Quote approval
5. Privacy and framing notes
6. Open items

Use these exact labels where they apply: Consent on file, Needs consent, Do not use, adult, minor, Approval status.

Do not rename, merge or skip a heading; if a section has nothing to report, keep the heading and say so in one line. Do not put square brackets or curly braces in headings or labels.

### Section contents

Write plain prose and tables. Do not use Markdown checkboxes or bracketed citations.

1. **Story bank** (table): Story ID | Person (changed name or role only) | Age group
   (adult or minor) | Source | Consent status (Consent on file, Needs consent, or Do
   not use) | What consent covers | Identification level | Next step.
2. **Consent actions**: for each Needs consent row, what to ask, of whom (participant,
   parent or guardian), and the organization's review step.
3. **Drafts**, one per requested format, using only Consent on file material, each
   headed with its format, word count, and the Story IDs used. Composites are labelled.
4. **Quote approval** (table): Quote | Speaker | Used in | Edited? | Approval status.
5. **Privacy and framing notes**: de-identification changes made, composite label
   used, framing changes from the request and why. Describe any rejected framing by its
   problem (for example "deficit-first opening", "clinical outcome claim") rather than
   reprinting it.
6. **Open items**: every item to confirm, every assumption, and who will resolve each.

## Quality checks

- Every person in the source material appears in the story bank with one consent
  status; nothing marked Needs consent or Do not use appears in a publishable draft.
- Every minor has parent or guardian consent and the organization's review recorded
  before any publishable draft uses their story.
- No detail, alone or combined, identifies a person beyond what their consent covers.
- Composites are labelled wherever they appear and carry no real photo or quote.
- The story is strengths-based: no opening at the lowest point, no rescue arc, no
  deficit language about the person or their community, no distress imagery.
- No clinical claims; outcomes are limited to what the program measured.
- Every quote is marked for quote approval.
- Fundraising versions use no guilt or fear framing and no false urgency.

## Boundaries

- The skill does not decide whether consent is legally sufficient. The organization's
  policy and counsel decide that, including for minors and for records that may be
  covered by FERPA (student education records) or HIPAA (health information); counsel
  determines whether either applies.
- It does not contact participants; staff who hold the relationship make consent and
  quote-approval asks.
- Commercial case studies go to the customer-case-study prompt; mental-health crisis
  wording to write-safe-mental-health-content; channel adaptation to repurpose-content.
