# Measurement and instruments

## Choosing a measure

For each outcome, decide:

1. **The construct:** knowledge, confidence (self-efficacy), attitudes or stigma, help-seeking intentions, reported help-seeking actions, sense of belonging, wellbeing, skill use.
2. **The respondent:** participant, parent or caregiver, facilitator, partner staff.
3. **The format:** existing validated instrument, adapted items, or items written for the program.

### Validated instruments

A validated instrument has been tested for whether it measures its construct consistently and meaningfully in a defined population. Use one when it fits the construct, age group, language, and length the setting allows.

Rules:

- Name an instrument only when you are certain of its name and what it measures. Examples of well-known non-clinical instruments in this area include the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (WEMWBS) and its short form (SWEMWBS), which measure mental wellbeing, and the General Help-Seeking Questionnaire (GHSQ), which measures intentions to seek help from different sources. Confirm age suitability, license or registration terms, and scoring rules from the instrument's own documentation before use.
- Do not state reliability coefficients, validity evidence, cut-off scores, or norms unless the organization supplies them from the instrument's documentation.
- Do not change wording, response scales, or item order of a validated instrument without noting that the adapted version is no longer the validated one.
- Score multi-item scales exactly as the instrument's documentation says; that is the case where averaging or summing items is appropriate.

### Instruments to avoid as program outcome measures

Clinical screening and diagnostic tools (for example, depression or anxiety screeners) are designed to identify people who may need clinical assessment. A non-clinical program should not use them as outcome measures:

- Results would be read as clinical change the program did not deliver and cannot claim.
- Some include items about self-harm or suicidal thoughts. A positive answer creates a duty to respond immediately, which requires clinical staff and a written protocol.
- Their use in schools may raise consent requirements (see `consent-privacy-and-ethics.md`).

If a partner requires such a measure, the partner's clinical staff should administer and hold it under their own protocol, and the program should not report it as a program outcome.

### Program-written items

When no validated instrument fits, write short, specific items tied to the curriculum:

- Knowledge: "Name two places on campus you could go for support" (open), or a multiple-choice item with one correct answer.
- Confidence: "How confident are you that you could help a friend find support?" with a labeled 4- or 5-point scale.
- Intentions: "If you were going through a hard time, how likely is it you would talk to...?" with a likelihood scale.
- Reported action (follow-up): "Since the workshop, have you...?" yes, no, prefer not to say.

Avoid double-barreled items, leading wording, and jargon. Include "prefer not to say" on sensitive items. Pilot with a few people from the audience. The survey-designer prompt can draft and refine item wording once the plan is set.

## Linking pre and post responses

- **Participant-generated code:** for example, first letter of a parent's first name + two-digit birth day + first letter of street name. Stable, not directly identifying, but check that it cannot be decoded easily in small groups.
- **System ID:** assigned by the survey tool from a roster; stronger linking but creates identifiable data that must be protected.
- **Unlinked:** anonymous waves. Report as two groups, not as individual change.

State the method in the plan and expect a share of codes not to match; report the matched count.

## Response rates

Response rate = completed responses / eligible participants invited x 100. State both numbers. Set the expected rate from the team's own past surveys; if none exist, label the planned rate "Assumed:" and review after the first cycle. Build response into the session (last 5 minutes) rather than relying on emails afterwards.

## Qualitative data

- Interviews or focus groups: use a short guide with open questions, record only with consent, and de-identify notes.
- Open-ended survey items: plan a codebook (code, definition, include, exclude, example) and have a second person code a subset to check agreement.
- Observation: a simple fidelity checklist of the session's core elements.
