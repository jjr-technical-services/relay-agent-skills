# Designs and proportionality

## Process and outcome evaluation

- **Process (implementation) evaluation** asks whether the program was delivered as intended, to whom, with what quality, and what affected delivery. Data: attendance, delivery logs, facilitator checklists, participant feedback, partner debriefs. It is almost always worth doing and is cheap.
- **Outcome evaluation** asks whether participants changed on the outcomes in the logic model. Its credibility depends on the design.

The CDC Framework for Program Evaluation (1999) is a commonly used structure: engage stakeholders, describe the program, focus the evaluation design, gather credible evidence, justify conclusions, ensure use and share lessons learned. This plan format follows that sequence.

## Designs and what each can support

| Design | How it works | Can support | Cannot support | Main threats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Post-only | Survey at the end | Participant satisfaction and self-reported learning; delivery feedback | Any claim of change over time | No starting point; social desirability |
| Retrospective pre-post ("then-test") | At the end, participants rate themselves now and, looking back, before the program | Self-perceived change; useful when participants cannot judge themselves well at the start (response-shift bias) | Objective change; recall is imperfect | Recall bias; desire to show improvement |
| Pre/post, same participants (matched) | Survey at start and end, linked by a participant code | Change among those who completed both | That the program caused the change | Maturation, outside events, attrition, testing effects, regression to the mean |
| Pre/post, unmatched | Anonymous surveys at start and end, not linked | Difference between two groups of respondents | Individual change; the groups may differ | Different people answer each wave |
| Pre/post with comparison group | A similar group not in the program is surveyed at the same times | Stronger evidence the change is related to the program | Causal proof unless groups are equivalent | Selection differences between groups |
| Randomized design | Eligible people are randomly assigned to program or not (or waitlist) | Causal claims about average effect, if well run and adequately sized | Generalization beyond the setting | Cost, ethics of withholding, sample size |
| Qualitative | Interviews, focus groups, open-ended items, observation | How and why, experience, barriers, unexpected effects | Prevalence or size of effects | Who chooses to speak; interpretation |

Decision rule for small programs: combine a process evaluation with either a matched pre/post or a retrospective pre-post on two or three short-term outcomes, plus a small qualitative component. Consider a comparison group only when a similar group is easy to reach (for example, a waitlist or a later cohort) and the decision justifies the cost. Randomization is rarely proportionate for a small team and needs an external evaluator.

Practitioners disagree on retrospective versus traditional pre-post. Retrospective pre-post reduces response-shift bias (people rating themselves higher at the start because they did not yet know what they did not know) and avoids linking problems, but relies on recall. Choose it when the program changes how participants understand the construct, or when linking pre and post is impractical. Choose traditional pre-post when the construct is concrete (knowing specific resources) and linking is feasible.

## Answering "prove impact"

When a funder asks the program to prove impact:

1. Clarify what they mean: many funders want evidence of outcomes and learning, not a causal study.
2. Offer the strongest proportionate design and state its claims honestly.
3. If they require causal evidence, describe what it takes (comparison or randomization, sample size, external evaluator, cost) so the decision is informed.

## Proportionality

Size the evaluation to:

- **Program scale and cost:** a one-hour webinar does not need a follow-up survey; a year-long cohort might.
- **Risk:** programs for minors or on sensitive topics need stronger protections, even at small scale.
- **Decision stakes:** renewal of a major grant justifies more effort than internal curiosity.
- **Capacity:** hours available, tools, skills.

Ways to stay proportionate:

- Use existing touchpoints (sign-in, closing minutes of a session) for data collection.
- Keep surveys short: often 5 to 10 items for a post-session form.
- Rotate deeper questions across cycles instead of asking everything every time.
- Sample for interviews (for example, 6 to 10 people) rather than interviewing everyone; stop when new interviews add little.
- Reuse the same items across programs so results can be compared.

Estimate hours per activity (setup, distribution and reminders per wave, cleaning, analysis, reporting) and compare with capacity. State what gets cut first if the total exceeds capacity.

## Attrition and matched pairs

Report how many completed the pre, the post, and both. Results based on matched pairs describe only those who completed both; if they differ from those who dropped out (for example, lower attendance), say so.
