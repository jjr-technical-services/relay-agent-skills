# Acknowledgment, receipts, restrictions and recognition

## Two different documents

- A **thank-you** is relationship communication. It should be prompt, personal and specific about what the gift supports.
- A **receipt** (in the US, the written acknowledgment the donor may need for a tax deduction) is a record with required content. One document can do both jobs if it contains the required content.

## US federal substantiation rules, stated carefully

These are general US federal rules. Confirm current rules, state rules and any unusual case with the organization's accountant before publishing a standard.

1. **Contributions of $250 or more.** A donor cannot claim a deduction for any single contribution of $250 or more without a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the organization. The acknowledgment should state: the organization's name; the amount of a cash contribution, or a description (not the value) of a noncash contribution; and whether the organization provided any goods or services in return, with a description and good-faith estimate of their value if it did (or a statement that only intangible religious benefits were provided, where that applies). "Contemporaneous" is tied to the donor's tax return timing, but the practical standard is to send it promptly after the gift. Separate gifts are generally not added together to reach $250; confirm special cases such as payroll deductions with the accountant.
2. **Quid pro quo payments over $75.** When a donor makes a payment of more than $75 that is partly a contribution and partly for goods or services (for example a gala ticket that includes dinner), the organization must give a written disclosure that tells the donor the deductible amount is limited to the amount paid above the value of the goods or services received, and gives a good-faith estimate of that value. The disclosure should be made in connection with the solicitation or the receipt of the payment.
3. **Low-cost items and certain membership benefits** can be disregarded under IRS rules within limits that are adjusted over time. Do not state thresholds from memory; ask the accountant for the current figures.
4. **Noncash gifts.** The organization describes the item; it does not value it for the donor. Higher-value noncash gifts can trigger additional donor and organization forms; route to the accountant.
5. **Donor-advised fund grants and matching gifts.** The legal donor is the fund or the company. Acknowledge the legal donor as required and thank the individual by soft credit. Do not provide benefits to the individual in exchange for a donor-advised fund grant without the accountant's review.

Never tell a donor how much they may deduct beyond the required disclosure language; that is the donor's and their adviser's determination.

### Illustrative quid pro quo arithmetic

Hypothetical round numbers: a donor pays $200 for a dinner ticket; the organization's good-faith estimate of the dinner's value is $80. The disclosure states that the amount of the contribution that may be deductible is limited to $200 − $80 = $120, and states the $80 good-faith estimate. The receipt must not describe the $200 as fully deductible.

## Acknowledgment standard

Set internal targets the organization can meet every time. A commonly used internal standard is to thank online gifts immediately by automated email and to send a personal acknowledgment within a few business days; treat that as a heuristic and set the organization's own targets by gift size and type.

| Gift type or size | Target time to thank | Channel | Signer | Receipt must include |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Any online gift | Immediate automated confirmation | Email | Organization | Organization name, date, amount, no-goods-or-services statement or benefit disclosure |
| First gift | Organization sets (for example within a week) | Letter or email plus a call for gifts above a set level | Named staff member | As above |
| Gift at or above the organization's personal-call threshold | Organization sets | Call, then letter | Executive director or board member | As above |
| Payment over $75 with a benefit | With the receipt | Letter or email | Organization | Quid pro quo disclosure with good-faith estimate |
| Noncash gift | Organization sets | Letter | Staff member | Description, no value stated by organization |
| Restricted gift | Organization sets | Letter | Program lead or executive director | Restriction restated in the donor's terms |
| Matching or donor-advised fund gift | Organization sets | Letter to legal donor, note to individual | Staff member | Legal donor named on receipt |

Signer rule: the more senior signer is a signal of importance only if it arrives on time. A late letter from the executive director is worse than a prompt one from a named staff member. If one signer is the bottleneck, add delegated signers by gift band.

## Restricted gifts and gift agreements

- A **restriction** is a donor-imposed limit on purpose (use for a named program) or time (use in a given year). The organization must use the funds as restricted or obtain the donor's permission, and in some cases legal process, to change the use.
- Record every restriction in the CRM and the accounting system with the same fund code. Finance releases restricted funds only when qualifying spending occurs.
- A **gift agreement** records purpose, amount, payment schedule, reporting commitments, recognition, and what happens if the purpose becomes impossible. Counsel drafts or approves the template.
- Stewardship of a restricted gift means reporting back on that purpose specifically. A general newsletter does not discharge a reporting commitment.

## Anonymity and recognition

- Record anonymity at the donor level and the gift level; a donor may want one gift anonymous and another credited.
- Anonymous donors are excluded from every public list, annual report, website, event program and signage. Internally, keep the record so the gift is receipted and stewarded.
- Ask for the donor's preferred recognition name. Do not infer it from the payment method.
- Use gift ranges in public lists unless the donor agreed to an exact amount. Check the list against the anonymity flag before every publication, and have a second person verify.
- Honor roll errors are relationship errors. Log each one and fix the rule that allowed it.

## Ethics that apply throughout

- Thank without guilt or pressure. Do not bundle a new ask inside a receipt unless the organization has chosen to and the donor has not asked to be excluded.
- Stories of people served require consent and de-identification; minors require parental consent and the organization's review. Use strengths-based framing.
- Never link donor records to program participation or service records.
