# Extracting requirements and reporting outcomes

## Where reporting requirements hide in a grant agreement

Read the whole agreement, the award letter, and any attachments. Requirements commonly sit in:

- the reporting schedule (interim and final due dates, and whether dates run from the award date or a fixed calendar);
- the funder's report template or portal questions;
- the approved proposal incorporated by reference (its objectives become the targets);
- special conditions (for example, "report the number of participants by county", "include a copy of any publication", "acknowledge the funder in materials");
- financial terms (report format, variance or rebudgeting rules, match documentation, unspent funds);
- publicity and acknowledgement clauses;
- data and privacy clauses (what may and may not be shared).

For US federal awards the agreement and 2 CFR 200 set performance and financial reporting requirements; follow the award's own terms and forms.

## Outputs versus outcomes

- **Output**: a count of activity or reach (workshops held, students attending, newsletters sent, partners engaged).
- **Outcome**: a measured change for participants or systems (knowledge gained, self-reported confidence, help-seeking intentions, a campus adopting a practice).
- **Impact** in the strict sense (a change attributable to the program compared with what would have happened otherwise) needs a comparison design; do not claim it from pre and post data alone. Hand evaluation design questions to design-program-evaluation.

## Outcomes vs targets

For each target:

`percent of target = actual ÷ target × 100`

Always show counts with percentages: "66% (264 of 400 respondents)". State the response count and response rate for survey measures; a change reported by a small share of participants is weaker evidence and should be described as such.

When a target was a percentage, report both the numerator and denominator. When the denominator changed (fewer participants), say how that affects the comparison.

## Writing a variance explanation

Four parts, in plain sentences:

1. **What happened**: the actual against target.
2. **Why**: the cause, categorized as timing, volume, external (a partner closure, a policy change), design (the approach did not work as planned), or data (the measure or collection failed).
3. **Effect**: on participants and on the grant's purpose.
4. **Response**: what the organization changed or will change, and whether a target revision needs the funder's approval.

Illustrative: "We delivered 30 of 40 planned workshops (75%). Two partner campuses paused student programming for a term. Completion per workshop held steady, so the shortfall is in reach, not engagement. We have rescheduled 8 workshops for next term and added a third campus partner; we ask to carry the remaining 2 into the next period."

Overshoots also get a line: a target exceeded by a wide margin can signal a target set too low or a measurement change.

## Data protection

- Report aggregates only. No names, student identifiers, dates of birth, or health information.
- Suppress any cell below the organization's threshold (state the threshold; if none is set, use a default and label it an assumption), and suppress complementary cells if the hidden value could be calculated from totals.
- Label suppressed cells "Suppressed" and explain once in a note.
- Student education records may be covered by FERPA and health information by HIPAA or state law; flag any data that might be, and leave applicability to the organization's counsel.
- Never link service use to donor or funder records.
