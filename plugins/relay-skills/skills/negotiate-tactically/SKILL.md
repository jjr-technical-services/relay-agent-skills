# Negotiate Tactically

## Overview

Help the user prepare, steer, and debrief negotiations with calm, ethical, high-leverage communication. Emphasize tactical empathy, active listening, calibrated questions, clear constraints, and durable agreements rather than pressure tactics.

Do not reproduce or summarize copyrighted book text. Use original phrasing and broadly applicable negotiation methods.

## Operating Principles

- Preserve trust and agency. Do not manipulate, deceive, threaten, fabricate alternatives, or hide material facts.
- Slow the interaction down when stakes are high. Help the user create room to think before making commitments.
- Diagnose before proposing. Identify the counterpart's interests, constraints, fears, deadlines, decision process, and hidden blockers.
- Prefer questions that make the counterpart explain how an agreement can work.
- Treat "no" as useful information, not failure. Use it to clarify boundaries and reduce defensiveness.
- Keep the user's walkaway point explicit. Do not optimize for agreement at any cost.

## Workflow

1. **Frame the situation**
   - Identify parties, stakes, desired outcome, current offer, deadline, leverage, constraints, relationship value, and BATNA or walkaway.
   - If key facts are missing, state assumptions and proceed with a draft that can be adapted.

2. **Map the counterpart**
   - Infer likely pressures, incentives, risks, objections, and decision criteria.
   - Separate stated positions from possible underlying interests.

3. **Choose the mode**
   - **Preparation**: Create a negotiation plan, discovery questions, likely objections, concessions, and exit criteria.
   - **Live response**: Produce concise wording for a call, email, text, or meeting.
   - **De-escalation**: Start with acknowledgment, labels, and clarifying questions before asks.
   - **Closing**: Confirm specifics, owners, deadlines, dependencies, and next steps.
   - **Debrief**: Analyze what happened, identify signals, and recommend the next move.

4. **Draft usable language**
   - Use short, natural phrasing.
   - Lead with acknowledgment when the counterpart is emotional or resistant.
   - Ask calibrated "how" and "what" questions that invite problem-solving.
   - Avoid yes-seeking pressure, ultimatums, over-explaining, and premature concessions.

5. **Provide a fallback**
   - Give the user a softer version, firmer version, or live-call version when the tone could materially change the outcome.
   - Identify the risk of each option.

## Core Techniques

### Labels

Name apparent emotions, concerns, or dynamics without asserting certainty.

Patterns:

- "It sounds like [concern]."
- "It seems like [constraint] is the main issue."
- "It looks like there is some hesitation around [topic]."

Follow a label with silence or a simple prompt. Do not stack labels into a speech.

### Mirrors

Repeat a few important words from the counterpart to invite elaboration.

Example:

- Counterpart: "The timeline is impossible."
- User: "Impossible?"

Use mirrors sparingly in written messages; they work best in live conversation.

### Calibrated Questions

Ask open questions that make constraints, tradeoffs, or implementation visible.

Useful forms:

- "What would need to be true for this to work?"
- "How should we handle [constraint]?"
- "What is the biggest blocker from your side?"
- "How would you compare this to the alternative you are considering?"
- "What part of this feels most difficult?"

Avoid questions that corner the counterpart or imply bad faith.

### Accusation Audit

When the user has bad news, a firm boundary, or a contentious ask, proactively acknowledge likely negative perceptions before making the point.

Use this when the user is raising a price, rejecting a request, enforcing a deadline, or reopening terms. Keep it brief and specific. Do not over-apologize or weaken the boundary.

### No-Oriented Questions

Use questions that make it safe for the counterpart to reject a premise.

Patterns:

- "Would it be unreasonable to..."
- "Is now a bad time to..."
- "Would you be opposed to..."

Use this to lower defensiveness, not as a gimmick.

### Summary and Confirmation

Before asking for commitment, summarize the counterpart's goal, constraint, concern, and agreed next step so they can correct or confirm it.
