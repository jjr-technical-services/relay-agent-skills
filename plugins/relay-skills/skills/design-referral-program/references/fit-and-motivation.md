# Fit test and referrer motivation

## 1. The fit test

A referral program works by making existing recommendations easier, more frequent, and trackable. Test for existing word of mouth before designing rewards.

### Evidence to collect

| Evidence | How to get it | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Share of new customers from personal recommendation | "How did you hear about us" answers (with response rate), signup source, CRM lead source, sales notes | Whether recommendations already drive acquisition |
| Count of promoters | NPS survey: share scoring 9 or 10, times the customer base, with response count | Pool of likely referrers (NPS = % promoters − % detractors, where detractors score 0 to 6) |
| Unprompted advocacy | Reviews, social mentions, community posts, customers introducing colleagues to sales | Willingness to recommend without a reward |
| Network relevance | Do customers know other potential buyers? (B2B: peers in the same role at other companies; B2C: friends with the same need) | Whether invites reach people who can buy |
| Product explainability | Can a customer describe the value in one sentence? Test with a handful of customer interviews | Whether a referral message will land |
| Referred-customer quality so far | Retention or repeat rate of customers who arrived by recommendation vs. others | Whether referrals bring good customers |

### Verdicts

Set thresholds with the user in advance, from their own data; there is no universal cut-off.

- **Go:** meaningful and stable share of new customers already from recommendation, a sizable promoter pool, and referred customers retain at least as well as others.
- **Pilot:** some organic referral activity but small numbers, or mixed satisfaction. Run a small, low-cost test with a holdout on the most satisfied segment.
- **Not yet:** little evidence of recommendation, low satisfaction, or high churn. Fix the product or onboarding first; a reward will attract low-quality or fraudulent referrals.

When no referral-source data exists, add a "how did you hear about us" or "who referred you" field and collect for a period before deciding. Say how long, based on the user's volume.

## 2. Who refers

Likely referrer segments, to confirm in the user's data:

- **Recent success:** customers who just achieved a meaningful outcome (first project delivered, first savings realized, first order received). Enthusiasm is highest shortly after.
- **Power users:** high usage frequency or depth.
- **Long-tenure customers:** trust is established, but enthusiasm may be lower; they respond to recognition and exclusivity.
- **Champions in business accounts:** the person who bought or implemented; recommending builds their reputation with peers.
- **Connectors:** people in roles with many relevant contacts (consultants, community organizers, agency staff). Consider whether they belong in a partner program instead.

Exclude or treat carefully: customers in a dispute or at churn risk, employees and their households (policy decision), and resellers.

## 3. Why people refer

| Motive | Description | Reward or mechanic that fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Altruism and helpfulness | Wants a friend or colleague to benefit | Double-sided reward where the referee gets the benefit; make the referee's offer the headline |
| Status and expertise | Wants to be seen as knowledgeable or early | Recognition, early access, badges, advisory boards, case-study invitations |
| Reciprocity and belonging | Feels part of a community | Community access, events, co-creation, charity donations in their name |
| Exclusivity | Enjoys giving access others cannot get | Invite-only access, limited guest passes |
| Money or value | Wants a tangible benefit | Cash, account credit, free product, discounts |

In B2B, many employees cannot accept personal cash from vendors under their employer's policies, and personal payments can look like a conflict of interest. Prefer rewards that benefit the account (credits, extended terms, added features), charitable donations, or recognition, and check the customer's likely gift policies.

Cash can crowd out the social motive: some people become less willing to recommend a friend when it looks like selling. Test framing ("give your friend a discount" vs. "earn money") rather than assuming.

## 4. The referee's barrier

The referee needs a reason to act now. Identify the main barrier (price, risk of trying, effort to start, uncertainty about fit) and choose a referee benefit that removes it: a discount for price, an extended trial or guarantee for risk, setup help for effort, or a personal introduction and a relevant example for fit.
