# Placement, red and green circles, pay transparency, and pay equity

## Placing current employees

For each employee, compute compa-ratio (salary ÷ midpoint) and range penetration ((salary − min) ÷ (max − min)), and classify:

- **Below minimum (green-circle)**: paid less than the range minimum.
- **Within range**: between minimum and maximum.
- **Above maximum (red-circle)**: paid more than the range maximum.

Before acting on any result, check that the employee is in the right job and level. Many apparent outliers are leveling errors, not pay errors. Fix the leveling first, then re-place.

Within range, many organizations expect position to reflect proficiency and sustained performance: newer or developing people in the lower third, fully proficient people around the midpoint, and consistently exceptional or highly experienced people in the upper third. This is a guide, not a rule; document the company's own intent.

## Green-circle handling (below minimum)

- **Cost to minimum** = sum over below-minimum employees of (minimum − salary), annualized. Add payroll-related costs (employer taxes, benefits that scale with pay) if the user wants a fully loaded figure, and label which figure is shown.
- Typical approaches: bring everyone to minimum at once; phase over two or more cycles with a published plan; or bring to minimum at the next review. Decision rule: if people are well below minimum or the gap correlates with a protected characteristic, fix sooner, and involve counsel.
- Check whether being below minimum is justified by being new to the level (for example, recently promoted). Even then, most organizations do not keep people below minimum for long, because the minimum is defined as the lowest acceptable pay for the job.

## Red-circle handling (above maximum)

- Do not cut pay. Common approaches: freeze base pay until the range moves above the salary; pay increases as lump sums that do not add to base; review whether the person's job has grown into the next level.
- Communicate the reason and the treatment to the employee clearly.
- If many people in one job are above maximum, the range may be too low or the market reference wrong; revisit the pricing.

## New hires and promotions

- Set a rule for new-hire placement (for example, up to the midpoint without extra approval, above midpoint with a documented reason and senior approval). Check new-hire offers against current employees in the same job and level to avoid paying new people more than proficient incumbents.
- For promotions, set a minimum increase and ensure the new salary is at least the new range minimum.

## Pay transparency

A growing number of jurisdictions require employers to disclose pay ranges in job postings, to applicants on request, or to employees, and some restrict asking candidates about pay history. The European Union has adopted a pay transparency directive that member states are implementing into national law. Requirements differ by country, state, and city in scope (employer size, remote roles), timing, and what "range" must mean.

What this skill does:

- Lists the jurisdictions where the company hires or employs people.
- Flags that transparency obligations may apply and must be confirmed with employment counsel.
- Checks that any range intended for posting is the range the company genuinely expects to pay for that role, not a stretched range to avoid disclosure.

What it does not do: state the current legal requirement in a jurisdiction as fact. Laws change frequently.

## Pay equity review

Pay equity means people doing substantially similar work are paid the same or differences are explained by legitimate, job-related factors. Steps:

1. **Group comparable employees**: same job family and level (and location tier where relevant).
2. **Identify legitimate factors** the company uses: level, location, time in level, relevant experience, performance. Document these before looking at results.
3. **Compare pay by group**: for small groups, compare individuals directly and review each unexplained difference. For larger populations, practitioners often use multiple regression of pay (commonly its logarithm) on the legitimate factors plus indicators for gender, race or ethnicity, or other protected characteristics where data is lawfully held. A statistically significant coefficient on a protected characteristic, after controlling for legitimate factors, indicates an unexplained gap to investigate.
4. **Beware tainted factors**: a factor like prior pay or performance ratings may itself carry bias. Test whether results change when such factors are excluded.
5. **Remediate** unexplained gaps, and fix the process that caused them (offer rules, promotion practices).
6. **Protect the analysis**: many organizations conduct pay equity analyses at the direction of counsel so that findings can be addressed under legal privilege. Let counsel decide how to structure it.

Only HR and authorized people should handle demographic data. Never include names or protected-characteristic data in outputs shared beyond them.

## Governance

- **Owner**: typically HR or a compensation lead, with leadership approval of the structure and budget.
- **Refresh cadence**: commonly annual, with market data re-aged and reviewed; more often in fast-moving labor markets.
- **Exceptions**: define who can approve pay outside range and how exceptions are recorded and reviewed.
- **Communication**: decide what employees see (their own range, all ranges, or the method) consistent with legal obligations and company policy.
