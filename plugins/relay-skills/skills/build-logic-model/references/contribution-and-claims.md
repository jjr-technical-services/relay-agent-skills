# Contribution, attribution, and claims

## Definitions

- **Attribution:** evidence that the program caused an observed change, with other explanations ruled out. This normally needs a design that shows what would have happened without the program: a randomized comparison, a well-matched comparison group, or a strong quasi-experimental design.
- **Contribution:** a reasoned, evidenced case that the program was one of the factors that helped produce a change. Contribution analysis, developed by John Mayne, sets out the theory of change, gathers evidence on each link, considers other influences, and states how confident the case is.

Most nonprofit programs, especially small ones, can credibly claim:

- Direct responsibility for outputs (they delivered the sessions).
- Short-term outcomes measured on participants, with the design's limits stated (for example, "participants reported higher confidence after the workshop than before" from a pre-post survey).
- Contribution, not attribution, for medium- and long-term outcomes.

## Building a contribution case

1. State the theory of change and its links (see `theory-of-change-method.md`).
2. For each link, gather evidence that it held: delivery data, participant feedback, follow-up responses, partner observations.
3. Identify other influences: other programs, policy changes, seasonal effects, general trends, and who else works on the same outcome.
4. Say how the evidence addresses those influences, and where it cannot.
5. Write the contribution statement: what changed, what evidence links the program to it, what else plausibly contributed, and how confident the organization is.

## Common overclaims and how to fix them

| Overclaim | Problem | Credible alternative |
|---|---|---|
| A partner's service usage rose, so the program caused it | Many factors change usage (staffing, awareness from other sources, enrollment, events); the data is not linked to participants | Report the partner trend as context. If participants report contacting a resource, report that as a participant outcome. |
| "Our program improves student mental health" | Clinical, population-level, and not measured | "Participants reported increased knowledge of support options and confidence in using them." |
| Percentages from a small or low-response survey presented as all participants | Nonresponse can bias results | State the number who responded and the response rate beside every percentage. |
| Pre-post gains described as caused by the program | Without a comparison, change could come from time, other events, or who answered | "Scores were higher after the program than before among respondents; this design cannot rule out other explanations." |

## Funder-safe language patterns

- "The program contributes to [long-term outcome] alongside [partners, services, policy]."
- "Among the [n] participants who completed both surveys, [x] percent reported..."
- "We track [context indicator] to understand the environment; changes in it reflect many factors and are not attributed to the program."
- "We will know this link is working if [indicator] reaches [proposed target] by [date]."

## When attribution is required

If a funder asks for proof of causal impact, explain what that would take (a comparison design, adequate sample, and usually an external evaluator), its cost relative to the program budget, and a proportionate alternative. Hand off to `design-program-evaluation` for the options.
