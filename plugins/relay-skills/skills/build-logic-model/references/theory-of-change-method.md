# Theory of change method

## Terms

- **Theory of change:** the explanation of how and why a set of activities is expected to lead to a desired change, including the preconditions and assumptions along the way. It can be a narrative, a diagram, or both.
- **Logic model:** a structured, usually one-page summary of the program in the order inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes. The W.K. Kellogg Foundation Logic Model Development Guide popularized this layout for nonprofits; many funders use a variant of it.
- **Precondition:** something that must be true before the next outcome can happen.
- **Mechanism:** the reason one step leads to the next (for example, practice builds confidence; knowing where to go reduces a barrier).

## Backward mapping

1. Write the long-term change as a condition, not an activity: "Students on partner campuses experience a campus culture where seeking support is common and accepted", not "Run awareness campaigns".
2. Ask "What must be true first for this to happen?" List the preconditions.
3. For each precondition, ask again. Stop when you reach a change the program's own activities directly produce.
4. Mark which preconditions the program works on and which it relies on others for (partners, services, policy). The second group becomes assumptions or external factors, not outcomes.
5. Only now list activities, and keep only those that produce a precondition in the chain. Activities that produce nothing in the chain are either missing an outcome or do not belong in this model.

## Outcome levels

| Level | Typical content | Typical timing | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outputs | Counts of what was delivered: sessions, attendances, materials, trained facilitators, partner sites | During delivery | The program |
| Short-term outcomes | Knowledge, awareness, attitudes, skills, self-reported confidence, intentions | At or soon after participation | Largely the program |
| Medium-term outcomes | Behaviors and practices: reported help-seeking actions, use of skills, peer support given, changes in partner practice | Months | Shared with participants and partners |
| Long-term outcomes | Conditions for a population or environment: culture, norms, policy, service access | Years | Many forces; the program contributes |

Timing ranges are common conventions, not rules; set them from the program's cycle (a one-time webinar has a shorter horizon than a year-long cohort).

## Writing causal links

Use: "If [precondition] then [result], because [mechanism]."

Good link characteristics:

- The result is observable (someone could collect data on it).
- The mechanism is stated and plausible.
- The link is small enough that one piece of evidence could support or weaken it.

Weak link: "If we run workshops then student wellbeing improves." Too large a jump, no mechanism, and wellbeing is not directly measured by attendance.

Stronger chain:

- L1: If students attend a workshop, then they can name campus and community support options, because the session walks through each option and how to access it.
- L2: If students can name support options and report more confidence in using them, then more will report intending to seek support when needed, because knowledge and confidence are two commonly studied barriers to help-seeking.
- L3: If more students intend to seek support and campus services are available, then more will report having contacted a support option, because intentions are more likely to become action when access is easy (assumption: services have capacity).

Label each link's evidence basis: research the organization can cite (the organization supplies the citation), program experience (for example, facilitator observation, prior feedback), or assumption. Do not invent citations.

## Assumptions and external factors

Assumptions to test commonly include: partners grant access; participants attend enough of the program to receive it; facilitators deliver it as designed (fidelity); materials are accessible and relevant to the audience; services participants are referred to exist and have capacity.

External factors commonly include: institutional policy changes, staffing changes at partners, service waitlists, economic pressures on participants, a major community event, and seasonal patterns (exam periods, school breaks).

For each, name a signal the team would notice and what they would do.

## Level of detail by use

- **Grant application:** match the funder's template and word limits exactly; one page of model plus indicators for the outcomes the funder asked about.
- **Board or strategy:** fewer boxes, clearer long-term change, explicit assumptions.
- **Evaluation planning:** full link list, indicators for every outcome, and data sources confirmed with the people who will collect them.
- **Staff onboarding:** plain language, activity-level detail, and why each activity matters.

## Common problems

- Outputs presented as outcomes ("500 students reached" is an output).
- Long-term outcomes the program cannot plausibly influence at its scale.
- Activities with no outcome, or outcomes with no activity.
- Many outcomes and no priorities; mark the two or three the program will be held to.
- A diagram with arrows that are never explained.
