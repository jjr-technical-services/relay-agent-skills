# Outcomes and indicators

## Non-clinical outcome wording

Programs that raise awareness, teach skills, build peer support, or connect people to resources are not clinical services. Their outcomes must describe what was measured, never a clinical result. The program did not diagnose anyone, so it cannot report changes in a condition.

| Wording to avoid | Why | Non-clinical alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced depression or anxiety | Implies clinical assessment and treatment effect | Increased self-reported confidence in managing stress; increased knowledge of coping strategies |
| Prevented suicides / saved lives | Cannot be measured by the program; implies causation of rare events | Increased knowledge of warning signs and how to connect a peer to support; increased reported intention to seek or offer help |
| Improved mental health | Too broad; implies clinical change | Increased self-reported sense of belonging or connection; wellbeing as measured by a named validated wellbeing scale, reported as self-report |
| Students got treatment | Implies the program arranged care | Increased share of participants who report contacting a support resource |
| Reduced crisis incidents on campus | Rare, influenced by many factors, and not program data | Partner reports on policy or practice changes; context only |

Rules:

- Say "self-reported" when the data comes from participants' own answers.
- Name the construct precisely (knowledge, confidence, intention, reported action, belonging).
- If the organization uses a validated wellbeing or help-seeking scale, name it only if the organization confirms it and follows the instrument's scoring and license terms. Do not state reliability or validity figures unless the organization supplies them from the instrument's documentation.
- Language is people-first ("students experiencing distress", "people who died by suicide"). Never "committed suicide".

## Indicator rules

An indicator is the specific measure that shows whether an outcome happened.

Each indicator needs:

| Field | Meaning | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Indicator | The measure, with its unit: "percentage of attendees who name two or more support resources on the exit form" | Restating the outcome ("improved knowledge") |
| Data source | Where the data comes from: attendance log, exit form, 3-month follow-up survey, partner report, facilitator observation checklist | A source nobody collects |
| Frequency | When it is collected: each session, each term, annually | Choosing monthly when the team can only manage once a term |
| Baseline | The value before or at the start of the program period, with date | Inventing a number; if none, write "Baseline needed" and when it will be collected |
| Target | The expected value by a date, set from the baseline and capacity | A round number with no basis; write "Proposed target" until leadership confirms |
| Owner | The role responsible for collecting and reporting | "Program team" with no single role |

Formula for a percentage indicator: numerator / denominator x 100, and state both. Example: participants who named two or more resources / participants who completed the exit form x 100. Report the denominator and the response rate so a high percentage from a few respondents is not mistaken for the whole group.

## Choosing indicators proportionately

- Prefer one strong indicator per outcome over five weak ones.
- Use data the program already touches (sign-in, exit forms) for outputs and short-term outcomes.
- Medium-term outcomes usually need a follow-up contact; plan for lower response and report it.
- Long-term outcomes often rely on partner or public data. Treat these as context indicators: report them, but do not present changes as program results.

## Small numbers and privacy

When indicators are reported by subgroup (campus, grade, identity), small groups can reveal individuals. Suppress any cell below the threshold the organization sets (a threshold such as 10 is a common choice; label whatever is used as an assumption until the organization confirms it). Participant-level data, student records, and health information stay out of reports.

## Output and outcome examples by program type (illustrative)

| Program type | Output | Short-term outcome indicator | Medium-term outcome indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workshop series | Workshops delivered; attendances | Percentage of exit-form respondents reporting increased confidence to support a peer (retrospective pre-post item) | Percentage of follow-up respondents reporting they used a skill from the workshop |
| Family program | Family sessions held; newsletter issues sent; resource page visits | Percentage of family respondents who can name a resource for their child | Percentage reporting a conversation about wellbeing with their child since the session |
| Youth advisory board | Meetings held; members retained | Members' self-reported confidence in advocacy skills | Number of youth recommendations adopted by the organization or partners |
| Campus partnership | Partner campuses; events co-hosted | Attendees' knowledge of campus resources | Partner practice or policy changes documented in partner meetings |
