# Plan Corporate Partnership

A corporate partnership works when both sides get what they said they wanted and neither side is surprised by the terms. Companies give for different reasons (community commitments, employee engagement, brand association, customer programs), and each reason brings different expectations, tax treatment and legal duties for the nonprofit. This skill finds partners whose own published priorities fit the mission, designs an exchange the organization can deliver, prices benefits honestly, spots where a sponsorship turns into advertising or a promotion turns into regulated cause marketing, and runs the partnership through activation, reporting and renewal. It supplies judgement and structure; the organization's accountant and counsel confirm tax and legal points.

## Inputs to gather

Ask for, or retrieve from the organization's context library. Proceed with labelled placeholders when something is missing and say what each placeholder changes.

- The organization's mission, programs open to partnership, and what it needs (unrestricted money, program funding, volunteers with specific skills, in-kind goods or services, reach).
- Gift acceptance policy and any partner screening criteria (industries or practices the organization will not associate with).
- For each prospect: the company's own published materials the user supplies or the agent retrieves and cites (impact or corporate social responsibility report, corporate foundation guidelines and deadlines, matching gift and volunteer grant policies, employee resource groups, local offices).
- Existing relationships: board members, donors, volunteers or alumni who work at the company; past gifts or matches from its employees.
- Any offer already on the table, with every benefit the company has asked for.
- Capacity to deliver benefits: events, recognition spaces, volunteer opportunities, staff time for reporting.
- Brand guidelines and the approval path for the organization's name and logo.
- Stories, images and data already approved for external use, with consent records.

Label every fact as **Supplied**, **Retrieved** (with source and date), **Calculated** or **Assumed**. Never invent a company's priorities, giving budget, deadlines, programs or contacts. If the company's materials are not available, say so and list what to look for.

## Workflow

1. **Screen before pitching.** Check each prospect against the gift acceptance policy and reputational risk criteria. Record any conflict (for example a product or practice at odds with the mission) and who decided.
2. **Map each prospect's stated priorities.** Quote or closely paraphrase, with source, what the company says it funds or cares about. Match those to specific programs. No source, no claim. Read `references/prospecting-and-value-exchange.md`.
3. **Score fit** on alignment with stated priorities, relationship strength, the company's giving mechanisms that fit the need, and risk. Pick a short list and a named relationship owner for each.
4. **Design the value exchange.** Decide which of the five forms apply (philanthropy, sponsorship, employee engagement, cause marketing, in-kind) and what each side gives and gets. Keep the exchange the organization can actually deliver.
5. **Build the benefit package** with a benefit value ledger: every benefit, its good-faith fair market value where it is a tangible good or service, its cost to deliver and its capacity limit. Read `references/benefits-tax-and-registration.md`.
6. **Test each benefit against the sponsorship rules.** In the US, a qualified sponsorship payment receives acknowledgment (name, logo, value-neutral product line mentions); qualitative or comparative claims, prices, endorsements or inducements to buy are advertising, which can change the tax treatment. Mark each benefit as acknowledgment, tangible benefit, or advertising-like, and send the list to the accountant.
7. **Check for cause marketing.** If a company will advertise that purchases benefit the organization, it may be a commercial co-venturer, and many states require registration, a written contract, disclosures or a final accounting. Rules vary by state; counsel checks every state where the promotion runs.
8. **Set brand-use approvals.** Limited, revocable permission to use each party's name and logo, approval before every use, turnaround time, and no implied endorsement of products. Participants' images and stories are never part of a brand license.
9. **Draft the agreement terms checklist** for counsel. Read `references/agreement-activation-renewal.md`.
10. **Plan activation** with a calendar, owners and approvals, including employee engagement events, volunteer screening where volunteers interact with young people, and content approvals.
11. **Plan reporting back** in the partner's own terms: what they said they cared about, what was delivered, what was measured, and what comes next. Aggregate data and suppress small groups.
12. **Plan renewal** well before the term ends: results against commitments, the partner's feedback, and a proposal for the next term. Record champions and plan for their departure.

## Decision rules where practitioners differ

- **Tiers or bespoke packages.** Use published tiers for events and recurring sponsorships with many sponsors; build bespoke packages for a small number of strategic partners. Either way, every benefit sits in the ledger.
- **Category exclusivity.** Offering to be the only sponsor in a category can be acceptable; agreeing that the organization will sell or promote only that company's products is a different and larger commitment. Treat the second as a counsel and accountant question.
- **Cause marketing or straight sponsorship.** Prefer a fixed sponsorship payment when the organization cannot monitor sales-based promotions across states; accept per-sale promotions only with a minimum guaranteed payment, a cap disclosed to consumers, and counsel's state review.
- **Employee volunteering.** Welcome it when it meets a real program need; decline or redesign volunteer days that cost more staff time than they return, and say so plainly to the partner.
- **Unrestricted versus program funding.** Ask first for unrestricted or broadly restricted support, and offer program naming only where the program can absorb the reporting.

## Designing employee engagement that helps

- Start from a real program need: a task volunteers can do well in the time they have, with supervision the staff can give.
- Size volunteer events to the program's hosting capacity, not to the company's team size; offer a second date or a remote task rather than overloading a session.
- Where volunteers may interact with young people, apply the organization's youth-protection rules (screening, supervision, no one-to-one contact) before the date is confirmed.
- Skills-based volunteering (for example finance, design or data help) needs a scoped brief, a staff owner and an end date.
- For workplace giving and employee resource group campaigns, confirm the company's approval, the matching policy from its own documents, and the tracking code, then hand the campaign to `plan-fundraising-campaign`.
- Credit matched gifts to the company, with soft credit to the employee, and never ask the company for employee personal data.
- Report volunteer hours and participation back to the partner in aggregate.

## Reviewing an offer a company has already made

When a company arrives with its own proposal, do not accept or reject it whole. Work through it line by line:

1. List every benefit and condition the company asked for, including ones stated only in email or conversation.
2. Enter each into the benefit value ledger and classify it.
3. Mark anything the organization cannot deliver, should not deliver (participant access, data, product endorsement), or cannot deliver without approval.
4. For advertising-like items, propose an acknowledgment alternative (logo and value-neutral product line mention instead of a slogan with claims).
5. For purchase-linked promotions, propose a fixed or minimum-guaranteed payment and flag the state review.
6. Present a counter-proposal that keeps what the company most wants and removes the risk, with the reason for each change in plain language.

## Illustrative example

Hypothetical round numbers: a company pays $20,000 and receives 8 event seats. The organization's good-faith estimate of each seat's dinner and entertainment value is $75, so tangible benefits total 8 × $75 = $600. The ledger shows $600 of tangible benefits against the $20,000 payment. If the company also asks for its slogan "the most trusted name in town" on the event banner, that slogan is qualitative and comparative, so the ledger marks it advertising-like and the plan either removes it or sends it to the accountant with the note that it can change the tax treatment of the payment.

## Common mistakes to catch

- Pitching a company on priorities it never stated.
- Agreeing to benefits no one has valued or costed.
- Letting sponsor copy include taglines, prices or calls to buy while calling the payment a sponsorship.
- Running a "portion of proceeds" promotion without a written contract, consumer disclosure of the amount, or a check of state co-venturer rules.
- Allowing a partner to use participant photos or stories, especially of minors.
- Sharing donor, participant or student data with a partner, even for matching or targeting.
- Reporting outputs the partner did not ask about while omitting the measures it did.
- Waiting until after the term ends to discuss renewal.

## Quality checks

- Every company priority has a source the user supplied or the agent retrieved and cited; nothing is inferred as fact.
- Every benefit appears in the ledger with a classification and, if tangible, a good-faith value; totals are rechecked.
- Qualitative, comparative, price or call-to-action language in sponsor copy is marked advertising-like and flagged.
- Any purchase-linked promotion is flagged for co-venturer review in each state involved.
- No participant images, stories or data are licensed or shared; any story used has recorded consent, with parental consent and organizational review for minors.
- Outcomes offered in reports are measured outcomes, never clinical or treatment claims.
- Screening results and conflicts of interest (for example a board member employed by the company) are recorded.

## Boundaries

- Tax treatment of sponsorship payments, unrelated business income, quid pro quo values and in-kind accounting are for the organization's accountant; the company's own tax treatment is the company's matter.
- Agreements, co-venturer registration, charitable solicitation registration, trademark licensing, volunteer liability and youth-protection requirements are for counsel.
- This skill does not decide whether the organization should associate with a company; leadership and, where policy requires, the board decide.
- Never promise a partner access to participants, donor lists or student data.
- Conflicts of interest, such as a board member or staff member employed by or invested in the partner, are handled under the organization's conflict of interest policy and recorded; the conflicted person does not approve the partnership.
- Crisis resources in any co-branded material use the organization's approved crisis resource copy verbatim.
