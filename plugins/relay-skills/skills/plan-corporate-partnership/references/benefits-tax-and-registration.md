# Benefits, fair market value, sponsorship rules and cause marketing registration

These notes describe US federal concepts and common state practice in general terms. The organization's accountant confirms tax treatment and counsel confirms legal requirements before any agreement is signed.

## Benefit value ledger

List every benefit the partner will receive. For each:

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Benefit | Exactly what is provided (for example "logo on event program", "8 dinner seats") |
| Classification | Acknowledgment, tangible benefit, or advertising-like (see below) |
| Good-faith fair market value | For tangible goods and services, what the item would cost to buy in an ordinary transaction; the organization's documented estimate |
| Cost to deliver | Direct cost and staff time |
| Capacity limit | How many can be provided in total across all partners |
| Approval needed | Brand, program, legal or accountant approval |

Fair market value (FMV) is what the benefit would sell for, not what it cost the organization and not the sponsorship price. Document how each estimate was made.

## Qualified sponsorship payments versus advertising

Under US federal tax law, a **qualified sponsorship payment** is a payment by a business for which there is no arrangement or expectation that the business will receive a substantial return benefit other than the use or acknowledgment of its name, logo or product lines in connection with the organization's activities. Such payments are generally not treated as unrelated business income.

Acknowledgment can generally include:

- the sponsor's name, logo, and slogans that do not contain qualitative or comparative descriptions;
- locations, telephone numbers and web addresses;
- value-neutral descriptions of the sponsor's product lines, including displays;
- brand or trade names.

Advertising-like content generally includes:

- qualitative or comparative language ("the best", "most trusted", "better than");
- price information or other indications of savings or value;
- endorsements;
- inducements to buy, sell or use the sponsor's products or services (for example offers, coupons, account-opening incentives).

Other features that can take a payment outside the qualified sponsorship definition, to confirm with the accountant: payments contingent on attendance, audience size or other measures of public exposure; an agreement that the organization will sell or promote only the sponsor's products (an exclusive provider arrangement, which differs from being the exclusive sponsor in a category); advertising in regularly scheduled periodicals; and some trade show arrangements. When a sponsor receives substantial benefits, part of the payment may be treated differently; the accountant allocates.

Practical rule: classify each benefit. If any are advertising-like, either remove them, redesign them as acknowledgment, or send the whole package to the accountant with a note that the tax treatment may change. Do not tell the partner how it may treat the payment on its own tax return.

## Tangible benefits and quid pro quo

When a partner receives goods or services such as event seats, meals or merchandise, those have a fair market value. If the payment is treated as a charitable contribution in part, the general US rule requires a written quid pro quo disclosure for payments over $75 that are partly a contribution and partly for goods or services, stating that the deductible amount is limited to the excess over the value received and giving a good-faith estimate of that value. The accountant decides how sponsorship and contribution rules interact for a given package.

Illustrative, hypothetical round numbers: 10 seats with a good-faith dinner value of $90 each = 10 × $90 = $900 of tangible benefits.

## In-kind contributions

The organization describes donated goods; it does not certify their value for the donor. Accounting rules recognize contributed services only when specific criteria are met, and contributed nonfinancial assets have their own presentation and disclosure requirements. The accountant decides recognition and valuation. Accept in-kind gifts only when the organization needs them and can use or store them.

## Cause marketing and commercial co-venturers

**Cause marketing** ties a donation to consumer purchases or actions ("a portion of each sale", "$1 for every account opened"). A for-profit company that advertises that purchases will benefit a charity is often called a **commercial co-venturer**. Many states regulate these promotions; requirements can include registration or notice by the company, a written contract with the charity, bonding, specific consumer disclosures, and a final accounting after the promotion. Requirements differ by state and change over time, so counsel reviews every state where the promotion is advertised or sold.

Regardless of state rules, good practice for the agreement and consumer-facing copy:

- state exactly how the donation is calculated (for example a fixed amount per unit or a percentage of a defined price);
- state any maximum or minimum, and the promotion's dates;
- set a minimum guaranteed payment so the organization is not exposed to a promotion that underperforms;
- require an accounting of units and amounts, and payment dates;
- give the organization approval of every use of its name and logo;
- make clear the organization does not endorse the product.

## Brand-use approvals

- License each party's name and logo for the named activities only, for the term, revocable on breach or reputational concern.
- Require written approval before each new use, with a stated turnaround.
- The organization's name must not appear in product claims or imply endorsement.
- Participant names, images, stories and data are never part of a brand license. Any story used by the organization itself needs recorded consent; minors need parental consent and the organization's review; use strengths-based framing and no distress imagery.
- Crisis resources in any co-branded content use the organization's approved crisis resource copy verbatim; never let a partner substitute its own.
