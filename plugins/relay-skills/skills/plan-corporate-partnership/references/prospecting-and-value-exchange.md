# Prospecting and value exchange

## Start from what the company says

Companies publish what they intend to support. Build the prospect list from those statements, not from company size or brand familiarity.

| Source | What to extract | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Impact, sustainability or corporate social responsibility report | Focus areas, communities, employee engagement goals, stated commitments | Reports describe past years; check the date |
| Corporate foundation guidelines | Eligible organizations, focus areas, geography, application process and deadlines, exclusions | Foundations are separate legal entities with their own rules; follow them exactly |
| Matching gift and volunteer grant policies | Ratio, caps, eligible organizations, submission deadlines, hours-for-dollars programs | Many are administered through third-party platforms; the organization may need to register |
| Employee resource groups | Communities the groups serve, heritage or awareness months, internal campaigns | Group campaigns usually need company approval |
| Local presence | Offices, facilities, employee concentration near programs | Local managers often have their own small budgets |
| Marketing and product | Customer segments that overlap with the mission | Raises cause marketing and brand-fit questions |
| Relationships | Board members, donors, volunteers, alumni employed there; employee gifts and matches in the CRM | Record conflicts of interest |

Every priority entered in the fit map carries its source and date. If a document is not available, write "Not found in supplied materials" rather than guessing.

## Screening

Apply the gift acceptance policy before outreach. Typical screening questions, adjusted to the organization's policy:

- Does the company's core business, marketing or conduct conflict with the mission or with the population served? For organizations serving young people, pay particular attention to products or marketing aimed at minors.
- Would the association expose participants, families or partners to pressure or unwanted marketing?
- Are there public controversies the board would want to weigh?
- Does anyone on the board or staff have a financial interest in the company? Record it under the conflict of interest policy.

Record the screening result, the decision and who made it.

## Fit rating

Rate each prospect on four factors, using a simple scale the team agrees (for example low, medium, high), with a sentence of evidence for each:

1. **Alignment:** stated priorities match a specific program.
2. **Relationship:** a real person connected to the organization can open the conversation.
3. **Mechanism:** the company has a way to give that fits the need (foundation grant, sponsorship budget, matching program, volunteer program, product donation).
4. **Risk:** screening concerns and delivery demands.

Prioritize high alignment plus an existing relationship. A large company with no stated alignment and no relationship is a long shot, whatever its size.

## The five forms of value exchange

| Form | What the company gives | What the organization gives | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Philanthropy | Grant or gift from the company or its foundation | Stewardship, reporting | Foundation rules; restrictions; benefits back to the company generally should be minimal |
| Sponsorship | Payment tied to an event, program or content | Acknowledgment; possibly tangible benefits | Qualified sponsorship versus advertising; tangible benefit values |
| Employee engagement | Volunteers, skills-based volunteering, workplace giving, matching gifts, employee resource group campaigns | Meaningful volunteer roles, giving campaigns, recognition of employees | Staff time; volunteer screening and supervision where young people are present; data handling for matches |
| Cause marketing | A donation tied to consumer purchases or actions | Use of name and logo in a promotion | Commercial co-venturer rules; consumer disclosures; brand approvals |
| In-kind | Products, services, space, media | Acknowledgment | Whether the organization truly needs it; valuation and accounting belong to the accountant |

Many partnerships combine forms. Keep each form's terms separate in the agreement so tax and reporting treatment stay clear.

## Designing the exchange

For each shortlisted partner, write:

- what the company said it wants (from its sources and conversations);
- what the organization needs;
- the smallest set of benefits that meets the company's stated aims;
- staff time to deliver, and who;
- what will be measured and reported, in the partner's terms;
- what the organization will not do (for example provide participant contact, endorse products, share data).

Hand off the resulting pitch to the corporate-partnership-one-pager prompt.
