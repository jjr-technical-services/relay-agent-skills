## 1. Evidence hierarchy

Use the strongest available evidence for the decision. Strength depends on proximity to the actual behavior, segment specificity, recency, sample quality, and freedom from selection bias.

| Evidence | Typical strength | Main limitation |
|---|---:|---|
| Paid transactions, renewals, churn, expansion | High | Existing customers reflect past targeting and product state |
| Product behavior and workflow observation | High | Usage does not always reveal motivation or willingness to pay |
| Win/loss records with reasons and alternatives | High-medium | Sales coding and attribution can be inconsistent |
| Recorded customer interviews about real events | Medium-high | Recall, courtesy, and interviewer bias |
| Support, implementation, and success records | Medium-high | Overrepresents customers with active issues or engagement |
| CRM pipeline and channel data | Medium | Process definitions and missing fields can distort patterns |
| Experiments with behavioral commitments | Medium-high | Tests one context and may have channel bias |
| Surveys | Medium-low | Stated preference is weaker than behavior |
| Internal stakeholder opinion | Low-medium | Useful for hypotheses, not customer proof |
| Public market reports and competitor pages | Contextual | Often broad, promotional, lagging, or definition-dependent |

Treat testimonials and anecdotes as signals until corroborated. Treat absence of complaints as weak evidence, not satisfaction.

## 2. Evidence inventory

Create an evidence register before drawing conclusions:

| ID | Source | Date/period | Segment | Sample | Observation | State | Limitation | Decision relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|---|

Use these states consistently:

- `observed`: behavior or outcome directly recorded;
- `reported`: a named source's account;
- `inferred`: analysis connecting evidence to a conclusion;
- `assumed`: unverified premise;
- `unknown`: missing evidence.

Flag evidence that is:

- older than the relevant product, pricing, market, or channel state;
- aggregated across materially different segments;
- based only on successful or vocal customers;
- collected after prompting respondents with the proposed answer;
- measured through a proxy that may not represent customer value;
- dependent on a single channel, salesperson, geography, or partner.

## 3. Research sequence

Use this sequence unless the decision requires a narrower method:

1. Define the decision and the minimum evidence needed to make it.
2. Audit existing sources before commissioning new research.
3. Form competing segment hypotheses, including a plausible null or status-quo choice.
4. Identify the assumptions that most affect the ranking.
5. Select the fastest credible method for each assumption.
6. Collect evidence without hiding contradictory cases.
7. Synthesize patterns by segment, trigger, alternative, and outcome.
8. Re-score segments and log what changed.
9. Set revision triggers for future data.

Prefer research that can change a decision. Do not collect broad background material when a small number of discriminating questions will resolve the ranking.

## 4. Customer interview guide

Interview recent buyers, lost prospects, churned customers, retained customers, heavy users, light users, and relevant non-buyers. Avoid using only friendly customers.

### Opening

- Establish the specific recent situation to discuss.
- Ask permission to probe the timeline and decisions.
- State that the product, not the participant, is being evaluated.

### Trigger and context

- "Take me back to when this first became important. What happened?"
- "What made that moment different from earlier?"
- "Who noticed the problem first?"
- "What would have happened if nothing changed?"

### Current behavior and alternatives

- "Walk me through how you handled it before."
- "What did that require in time, money, risk, or coordination?"
- "What other approaches did you seriously consider?"
- "Why was continuing with the status quo still attractive?"

### Buying process

- "Who had to agree, approve, evaluate, or implement?"
- "What criteria mattered to each person?"
- "What nearly stopped the decision?"
- "What evidence reduced the perceived risk?"

### Value and outcome

- "What changed after adopting the solution?"
- "How do you know?"
- "Which outcome mattered most, and to whom?"
- "What remains unresolved or disappointing?"

### Language

- Capture exact phrases sparingly and only when source permission and policy allow.
- Distinguish participant language from researcher paraphrase.
- Do not ask "Would you use/buy this?" as the primary evidence of demand.

### Interview record

Record participant segment, role, lifecycle state, date, relevant event, direct observations, quotations where permitted, contradictions, and follow-up questions. Avoid including unnecessary personal or sensitive data.

## 5. Market and competitor research

Research the alternatives customers actually use:

- direct competitors;
- manual workflows and spreadsheets;
- agencies, consultants, or internal staff;
- adjacent platforms;
- delaying or doing nothing.

For each alternative, assess target, category frame, promised outcome, mechanism, proof, pricing model if public and relevant, switching friction, and gaps. Use current primary sources where possible: product documentation, pricing pages, customer stories, filings, or original research. Date volatile facts.

Separate:

- competitor self-description;
- verified capability;
- customer perception;
- the proposed differentiation.

Never infer market demand solely from competitor count or ad activity.

## 6. Synthesis and confidence

Cluster evidence by the causal pattern that matters:

`situation -> trigger -> struggle -> alternative -> decision criterion -> adoption -> outcome`

For every major conclusion, record:

- supporting and contradicting evidence;
- affected segment and context;
- confidence from 0 to 1;
- implications for ICP, product, channel, or UVP;
- the next evidence that would increase or decrease confidence.

Suggested confidence anchors:

- `0.10-0.30`: assumption or weak anecdotal signal;
- `0.40-0.60`: repeated reported pattern with material limitations;
- `0.70-0.85`: converging behavioral and qualitative evidence;
- `0.90-1.00`: strong, recent, replicated evidence for the stated scope.

Do not use `1.00` merely because data exists. Confidence is conditional on the scope and measurement quality.

## 7. Research failure modes

- Starting with persona adjectives instead of a buying situation.
- Interviewing only customers who already like the product.
- Treating stakeholder consensus as external validation.
- Averaging segments that have different triggers or economics.
- Asking leading questions that repeat the proposed value proposition.
- Converting feature requests into evidence of willingness to pay.
- Using total-addressable-market estimates to choose a reachable beachhead.
- Ignoring lost, churned, low-usage, or failed-implementation cases.
- Quoting customer language without preserving its context.
- Declaring validation without a threshold or decision rule.
