# Competitive Research Method

Use this method to build a current, buyer-centered competitive snapshot from a URL, name, category description, or upstream brief.

## Contents

- Set the scope
- Crawl the target
- Discover competitors
- Build the source plan
- Capture comparable observations
- Assess channel posture
- Synthesize without overclaiming

## Set the scope

Define the buyer decision before searching:

`buyer or user + job or problem + trigger + budget or resource + geography + decision horizon`

The competitive set changes with scope. A global enterprise platform, local consultant, internal team, and spreadsheet may all compete for different versions of the same job.

State an evidence cutoff date. Revisit time-sensitive facts such as pricing, product scope, leadership, partnerships, ad activity, platform availability, and campaign messaging.

## Crawl the target

For a supplied URL:

1. Open the exact page and establish the canonical business or offer.
2. Follow decision-relevant first-party links: homepage, product or service, solutions, industries, pricing or packages, customers or case studies, comparison pages, integrations or partners, resources, and about.
3. Record the wording and structure of the category, audience, problem, promise, mechanism, proof, offer, and CTA.
4. Note message variants by segment or page; do not flatten them into one claim.
5. Stop when additional pages are unlikely to change the competitor set or comparison.

Use the sitemap or external search when normal navigation is incomplete. Do not bypass access controls or crawl indiscriminately.

## Discover competitors

Search by the buyer's language and alternatives, not just the target's category label. Useful query patterns include:

- `[job/problem] software`, service, agency, consultant, platform, or alternative;
- `[target] alternatives`, versus, compare, pricing, reviews, customers;
- `[category] for [segment or industry]`;
- `[trigger] solution` or `[current workaround] replacement`;
- regional or regulated variants where geography changes the decision.

Build a candidate set, then select four to eight competitors representing the decision:

| Type | Selection reason |
|---|---|
| Direct | Same job and similar audience |
| Specialist | Strong fit for a priority segment, use case, or geography |
| Adjacent | Different approach that can absorb the same budget |
| Substitute | Internal, manual, service, or bundled alternative |
| Status quo | Delay, do nothing, or retain the current workflow |
| Emerging | New entrant changing buyer expectations |

Do not choose only the most visible brands. Explain inclusion and material exclusions.

## Build the source plan

Use source types for the claims they can support:

| Source | Appropriate conclusion | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Competitor site and docs | What the company claims, offers, prices, or demonstrates | Self-reported and page-specific |
| Case study or testimonial | Reported outcome for a named example | Selective, often uncontrolled |
| Product or trial | Observable workflow or capability | May vary by plan, setup, or date |
| Regulatory or public record | Registration, filing, certification, or official event | Narrow scope |
| Independent research or reporting | Market context or verified event | Method and recency vary |
| Reviews and forums | Reported user perception and language | Selection bias and unverifiable identity |
| Search results and content corpus | Discoverability, themes, publication activity | Rank and volume are not business performance |
| Ad library or social profile | Visible active creative, cadence, message, format | No reliable spend, targeting, conversion, or profitability |
| Jobs, partners, integrations, events | Capability or distribution signals | Strategic importance remains inferred |

For important conclusions, prefer first-party evidence plus one independent or behavioral signal when practical. Cite the exact supporting page, not a search-results page.

## Capture comparable observations

Use a consistent record for each priority competitor:

```yaml
competitor_observation:
  name: ""
  type: direct, specialist, adjacent, substitute, status-quo, or emerging
  observed_at: YYYY-MM-DD
  target_and_trigger: ""
  category_frame: ""
  promised_outcome: ""
  mechanism: ""
  proof: []
  offer_and_pricing: ""
  primary_cta: ""
  conversion_path: ""
  channel_signals: []
  strengths: []
  gaps_or_contradictions: []
  evidence_state: observed, reported, inferred, assumed, or unknown
  sources: []
```

Use short paraphrases. Quote only when exact language is strategically important and keep excerpts brief.

## Assess channel posture

For each observable channel, capture:

- surface and audience hypothesis;
- first and latest visible activity dates;
- cadence or volume only when actually measured;
- recurring themes, formats, proof, offers, and CTAs;
- destination and conversion path;
- relationship to product, partner, event, content, or sales motions;
- evidence limitations.

Conclude only what the evidence supports:

- Safe: “The company published eight buyer guides between May and July and routes them to a demo.”
- Safe inference: “This is consistent with an education-led demand-capture motion.”
- Unsafe: “SEO is their best channel.”
- Unsafe: “The repeated ad must be profitable.”

Frequency can indicate emphasis or testing. It cannot establish success without performance data.

## Synthesize without overclaiming

Map patterns across:

`target + trigger + category + promise + mechanism + proof + offer + CTA + channel posture`

Identify:

- positions strongly occupied by credible proof;
- generic claims repeated without distinctive mechanisms;
- segments, triggers, outcomes, proof types, offers, or journeys underserved in accessible evidence;
- competitor advantages the target must neutralize;
- category conventions buyers may rely on;
- channel saturation or distribution asymmetry;
- unknowns requiring interviews, product trials, sales evidence, or campaign tests.

Test whitespace against buyer value, target capability, proof, economics, and competitive response. Empty space alone is not opportunity.
