# Calibration, mapping existing employees, and promotion

## Calibrating the draft with real examples

A ladder is not finished until it has been tested against real work. Procedure:

1. **Collect examples.** Ask managers for short, anonymized descriptions of specific work (a project, a decision, an incident handled, a customer situation) by people broadly regarded as performing solidly at each level. Remove names and identifying details. Aim for several examples per level; include some borderline cases.
2. **Blind placement.** Give the draft ladder and the examples, without the current level, to several reviewers from different teams. Each assigns a level and cites the ladder wording that decided it.
3. **Compare.** Where reviewers agree, the wording works. Where they disagree, find the cell that caused the disagreement and rewrite it to separate the levels in kind.
4. **Repeat** with new examples until agreement is consistently high. A simple measure is the share of examples on which all reviewers agree on the level; track it across rounds. The threshold is the organization's decision; state it.
5. **Record** the examples (anonymized), results, and wording changes. Keep a small set of calibrated examples as reference cases for future promotion panels.

If no real examples are available, publish the ladder marked "uncalibrated" and schedule calibration before it is used for promotion or pay decisions.

## Mapping existing employees

Mapping is where ladders succeed or cause lasting damage. Principles:

- **Separate mapping from pay.** Announce that mapping determines level; pay alignment follows under a separate process with its own timeline (design-comp-bands). People evaluate the mapping more honestly when pay is not decided in the same conversation.
- **Evidence-based.** Each manager proposes a level for each report, citing specific recent work against the ladder, not tenure or title.
- **Cross-team calibration panel.** Managers present proposals to a panel covering several teams. The panel looks for consistency: are people doing similar work at similar levels across teams?
- **Title changes.** If mapping changes someone's title, decide in advance how it is communicated, and whether existing titles are preserved for a transition period.
- **Down-leveling.** If a person's current title implies a higher level than the evidence supports, handle with care: consider keeping their title and pay (red-circle treatment for pay) while being clear about the level and the path forward. Involve HR.
- **Appeals.** Provide a route for employees to ask for a review with additional evidence.
- **Disparity review.** After mapping, HR should check outcomes by demographic group for unexplained patterns before results are finalized.

## Promotion process

Define each element explicitly:

| Element | Decision to make |
|---|---|
| Eligibility | Any minimum time in level (if any), and whether promotions happen in cycles or any time |
| Nomination | Manager, self-nomination, or both |
| Evidence standard | Sustained work at the next level's expectations, over a period the organization sets, across the ladder's dimensions |
| Evidence packet | Specific work examples mapped to next-level expectations, peer and stakeholder input, manager assessment |
| Decision body | Calibration panel or committee with members from outside the immediate team |
| Consistency check | Comparison with reference cases and other candidates in the cycle |
| Outcome communication | Decision, reasons tied to the ladder, and for unsuccessful cases specific evidence that would change the outcome |
| Pay | Promotion increase rule and minimum placement in the new band |

Principles:

- Promote for work already being done at the next level, not for potential alone. Some organizations promote on demonstrated readiness plus a short stretch; state the rule.
- Avoid rules that depend on opportunity alone (for example, "must have led a launch"); a manager's job includes giving people the opportunity to demonstrate next-level work.
- Do not use promotion as a retention tool for people not performing at the next level; that damages the ladder's credibility. Use pay within band instead.
- Track promotion rates and time-in-level by team and demographic group with HR, and investigate unexplained differences.

## Maintenance

- Owner: typically HR with a senior leader from the family.
- Review: at least annually, and whenever the organization's structure changes substantially.
- Change process: proposed wording changes are calibrated against reference cases before adoption.
- Communication: publish changes with the reason, and state whether they affect anyone's level.
