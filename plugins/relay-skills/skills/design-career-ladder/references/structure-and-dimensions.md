# Ladder structure: tracks, levels, and dimensions

## Tracks

**Individual contributor (IC) track**: progression through expanding scope of personal and technical or functional contribution, including influence without formal authority.

**Manager track**: progression through expanding responsibility for people, teams, and organizational outcomes. Managers are accountable for hiring, developing, and retaining people, and for their team's results.

Design decisions:

1. **Branch point.** Choose the first level at which someone can move onto the manager track. People usually need to have performed well as a senior individual contributor before managing others in the same craft. Decision rule: branch at the level where a person typically leads work across others informally; do not create a manager track below it.
2. **Equivalence.** Map each manager level to an IC level with the same compensation band. If the IC track stops lower than the manager track, the organization is signaling that the only way to grow is to manage, which pushes strong experts into management they may not want.
3. **Movement between tracks.** State that moving from manager to IC (or back) at an equivalent level is a lateral move, not a demotion, and how it is handled.
4. **Single-track families.** Small families, or families where management is rare, can use one track with a note that people management is a separate role at a stated level.

Some organizations add a third track for technical leads who guide the work of a team without people management. Decision rule: add it only if the organization has that role at scale and it differs materially from both IC and manager expectations; otherwise express it as IC leadership expectations.

## Number of levels

Considerations:

- **Distinct work**: each level should correspond to a recognizable step in scope or type of work.
- **Time in level**: people need room to grow within a level before moving; too many levels makes each promotion small and frequent, and turns the ladder into a tenure clock.
- **Organization size**: a small company does not need levels for roles it will not have for years. Define levels in use plus one above.
- **Alignment across families**: if the company runs one leveling scheme across families (so level 4 in engineering is comparable in scope to level 4 in finance), the level count and the meaning of each level number must be consistent.

Terminal level: define the level at which people can comfortably remain for a career without expectation of promotion (often the first senior level). Levels below it are "growth levels" where progression is expected within a period the organization sets; say so, so that staying at a growth level indefinitely is handled as a performance matter rather than an ambiguity.

## Dimensions

Dimensions are the lenses used to describe every level. Choose four to six. Define each so it means the same thing at every level. A common set:

| Dimension | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Scope | What does this person own: a task, a feature, a system, a product area, a function? |
| Impact | Who and what changes because of their work, and how far does it reach: their team, several teams, the business, customers? |
| Complexity | How ambiguous and interdependent are the problems they solve? Are problems defined for them, or do they define the problems? |
| Autonomy | How much direction do they need, and how often do they check in before deciding? |
| Leadership and influence | How do they improve others' work: through review, mentoring, setting standards, leading initiatives, building teams? |
| Craft | The functional expertise of the family (for engineering, technical design and quality; for sales, deal strategy and forecasting; for finance, accuracy and judgment in accounting and analysis). |

Tailoring: for some families, add a dimension that captures what distinguishes seniority there, such as "customer and stakeholder relationships" for account management or "commercial judgment" for sales. Do not add dimensions that do not distinguish levels.

Weighting: at lower levels, craft and delivery dominate; at higher levels, scope, impact, and influence dominate. Some ladders make this explicit by describing which dimensions matter most at each level. This is a common pattern, not a rule; state the organization's choice.

## Titles

Titles are external labels; levels are internal definitions. Decide:

- One title per level per track (for example, Engineer, Senior Engineer, Staff Engineer).
- Whether titles are shown externally for every level or grouped.
- Avoid title inflation: a title that implies a level the person has not reached creates problems at the next hiring or promotion decision.

## Cross-family consistency

When several families share a leveling scheme, write a short "universal level guide" first: one paragraph per level describing scope, impact, and autonomy in family-neutral terms. Each family ladder then adds craft-specific detail. This keeps level 4 in marketing comparable to level 4 in engineering for pay and for internal transfers.
