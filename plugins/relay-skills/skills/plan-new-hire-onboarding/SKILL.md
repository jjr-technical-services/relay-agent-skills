# Plan New Hire Onboarding

Onboarding is the period between signed offer and the point where a new employee works independently at the level they were hired for. Done well, it can shorten the time to real contribution and reduce avoidable early departures; done badly, the new hire spends weeks waiting for access, reading documents nobody maintains, and guessing what success looks like. This skill produces two things: a plan for one person in one role, with outcomes specific enough to judge at day 30, 60, and 90, and a reusable program template a team can run for every hire. The plan is built backward from what the role must deliver, not forward from a generic checklist.

## Inputs to gather

- **Role**: title, level, team, and the two or three outcomes the role exists to deliver in its first year. The job description and the level expectations (from design-career-ladder, if available) are the best sources.
- **New hire context**: start date, location or remote, time zone, prior experience relevant to the role (from the hiring process), and any stated development areas the interviewers noted.
- **Team context**: manager, likely buddy, key stakeholders inside and outside the team, current priorities, and a list of real work that could be a first meaningful contribution.
- **Systems and access**: the tools the role uses, who grants access to each, and lead times.
- **Company requirements**: required training (for example, security awareness, harassment prevention, role-specific compliance), payroll and benefits enrollment steps, equipment process.
- **Constraints**: manager availability in the first weeks, holidays, team events, cohort onboarding sessions.

If the role outcomes are unknown, ask the hiring manager. If they cannot say what success at 90 days looks like, flag that as the first problem to fix; use clearly labeled placeholder outcomes until they do.

## Workflow

1. **Define what "productive" means for this role.** Write the role's first-year outcomes, then work backward to what must be true at day 90, 60, and 30. Read `references/role-outcomes-and-contribution.md`.
2. **Choose the first meaningful contribution.** Pick a real, bounded piece of work that the new hire can ship in the first weeks, that matters to someone, and that exposes them to the team's tools and people. It should be scoped so it can succeed with support.
3. **Plan pre-start.** Equipment ordered, accounts requested with lead times, paperwork sent, buddy assigned, calendar holds for week one, a welcome note from the manager, and first-week schedule shared. Read `references/phases-and-cadence.md` for the full checklist.
4. **Plan day one.** Working equipment and access, a welcome from the manager in person or on video, the buddy introduced, the 30/60/90 plan reviewed together, and at least one small real task. Keep administration to what is required on day one.
5. **Plan week one.** Stakeholder introductions with a purpose for each, required training, the setup of the development environment or core tools, shadowing, and starting the first contribution.
6. **Write the 30/60/90 outcomes.** Each outcome is observable and specific to the role. Pair each with how it will be checked.
7. **Set the learning plan.** What the new hire must learn (product, customers, processes, systems, domain), in what order, from which source or person, and how they will know they have learned it.
8. **Set the cadence.** Manager one-to-ones (weekly at least in the first 90 days is common practice), buddy check-ins, and formal checkpoints at 30, 60, and 90 days with two-way feedback.
9. **Define success measures.** For the individual: 30/60/90 outcomes met, time to first contribution, time to productivity as defined in step 1. For the program: new-hire feedback, time to productivity across hires, and early retention. Read `references/program-template-and-measures.md`.
10. **Produce the manager's checklist** and, if requested, the reusable team template.

## Worked example (illustrative, hypothetical role)

Role: mid-level customer success manager joining a team that manages renewals for mid-sized accounts.

| Checkpoint | Outcome (observable) | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| Day 30 | Has met each of the 10 accounts in their book with the outgoing owner, and written a one-page account plan for each that the manager has reviewed | Account plans in the shared folder; manager review notes |
| Day 60 | Runs renewal conversations for accounts renewing in the next quarter, with the manager attending the first two | Call notes; manager feedback |
| Day 90 | Owns the book independently: forecast submitted on time with stated risk for each account, and at least one expansion or risk-mitigation plan agreed with a customer | Forecast submission; account notes |

First meaningful contribution (week 2): update the renewal risk notes for three accounts after shadowing their calls, reviewed by the buddy. The numbers here are hypothetical; set the book size and timing from the team's actual work.

## Sample day one shape (adapt times and people)

| Block | Activity | With | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | Welcome, agenda, walk through the 30/60/90 draft | Manager | Clarity on the first week and what success looks like |
| Morning | Equipment and access check | IT and buddy | Fix anything broken the same day |
| Midday | Team introduction or shared lunch | Team | Put names to faces |
| Afternoon | Tour of tools and where information lives; first small real task | Buddy | Early accomplishment using real tools |
| Afternoon | Required day-one administration only | HR | Meet obligations without crowding out the rest |
| Close | Check-in and plan for day two | Manager | Answer questions; confirm tomorrow |

For remote hires, keep the same blocks on video, with shorter sessions and breaks between them.

## Adjusting for the situation

- **Remote hires**: ship equipment early enough to arrive before day one and plan a setup call; schedule more deliberate introductions, because hallway contact does not happen; agree core overlap hours across time zones; use video for the manager welcome and early one-to-ones.
- **Senior hires and leaders**: the first 30 days lean toward listening and diagnosis (meetings with stakeholders, reviewing plans, understanding the team) before changing direction; the 90-day outcome is often a written assessment and plan agreed with their manager, plus early decisions they own.
- **New managers**: add meetings with each direct report in week one, a handover of each person's goals and development notes from the previous manager, and the team's operating rhythm; include the company's manager training.
- **Handover roles** (taking over a book of accounts, a system, or a process from someone leaving): schedule overlap with the outgoing person early, capture open commitments and risks in writing, and introduce the new owner to customers or stakeholders jointly.
- **Internal transfers**: skip company basics, keep role-specific outcomes, introductions, and access; do not assume they already know the new team's norms.
- **Cohort starts**: combine company-level sessions across the cohort; keep the role module individual.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Starting access requests on day one, so the first week is spent waiting.
- A 30/60/90 plan made of activities ("attend training", "meet the team") instead of outcomes.
- No real work in the first two weeks, which delays learning and signals the hire is not needed yet.
- Overloading week one with documents to read and no task that uses them.
- The manager delegating onboarding entirely to the buddy or HR.
- No checkpoint until the 90-day mark, so problems surface too late to fix.
- Asking the new hire for feedback and not acting on it.

## Output

1. **Summary**: role, start date, manager, buddy, definition of productive for this role, first meaningful contribution.
2. **Pre-start checklist**

| Item | Owner | Due (days before start) | Lead time | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|

3. **Day one schedule**: time, activity, with whom, purpose.
4. **Week one plan**: by day, with stakeholder introductions and training.
5. **30/60/90 outcomes**

| Checkpoint | Outcome | How it is checked | Support provided | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|

6. **Learning plan**

| Topic | Why it matters for the role | Source or person | By when | How mastery is shown |
|---|---|---|---|---|

7. **Stakeholder introductions**

| Person or role | Purpose of meeting | Questions to ask | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|

8. **Cadence**: manager one-to-ones, buddy check-ins, formal checkpoints, and feedback questions.
9. **Success measures**: individual and program-level, with definitions.
10. **Manager's checklist** by phase.
11. **Reusable team template** (when requested): the same sections with role-specific fields left as prompts.
12. **Assumptions and open items.**

## Quality checks

- The 30/60/90 outcomes are specific to the role and observable; none could be copied unchanged into a different role's plan.
- The first meaningful contribution is real work with a named beneficiary, scoped to succeed in the first weeks.
- Every access and equipment item has an owner and is requested early enough for its lead time.
- Day one includes working equipment, a manager conversation, and a buddy introduction, not only administration.
- Each stakeholder introduction has a stated purpose.
- The cadence includes two-way feedback: the new hire gives feedback on onboarding, not only receives it.
- Time to productivity has a definition for this role before it is measured.
- Required compliance training is listed without inventing legal requirements; the company's own list is used.
- Nothing personal about the new hire beyond what is needed for the plan is included.

## Boundaries

- Employment paperwork, right-to-work verification, payroll, benefits, and required training are governed by law and company policy; HR owns them. This skill lists them as checklist items and does not advise on legal requirements.
- Accommodations for disability or other needs are handled through HR's process, confidentially.
- The hiring manager owns the plan and the 30/60/90 judgments; the plan supports, not replaces, the manager's judgment.
- Job descriptions come from job-description-writer; interview design from structured-interview-kit; level expectations from design-career-ladder.
- This skill is for employees. For onboarding customers or product users, use a product or customer onboarding approach instead.
